# The Migratory Body

The migratory body is a body in transit, displaced or wandering, embodying forced or chosen mobility. The migratory body is a bridge between worlds, memory and otherness in motion.

For conquered peoples, the meaning of displacement radically changed. Nomadism—once guided by logics of community sustainability and balance with the environment—was transformed into migration, associated with uprooting, banishment, and violence.

Coloniality has since defined who may legitimately move and who may not, applying stigmas inscribed in Western imaginaries: migratory populations from the Global South are criminalized, reduced to threats, stripped of the right to travel. The denial of that right translates into criminalization: whoever crosses without permission is “illegal,” a “criminal.”

Thus, the migratory body is not merely one who crosses territories, but a figure marked by the violences produced by borders, economies, and wars. For those at the margins, migration may be choice or necessity, but it almost always occurs within an unequal geopolitics that classifies bodies, passports, and lives as legitimate or illegitimate, placing every displacement under suspicion.

These bodies in transit become fabricated otherness: seen as strangers, threats, or excess by states that regulate circulation, they embody the historical continuity of peoples dispossessed, enslaved, or pushed into diaspora.

But these bodies in transit are above all memory in movement—carriers of languages, affects, and cosmovisions that overflow the idea of a homogeneous nation. They are a bridge between worlds, a space of mestizaje, and the fissure that questions the rigidity of political and cultural borders.

In Pangea, migratory bodies are symbols of interconnection and transit : they remind us that every territory is historical and mutable, that every identity is mobile, and that life is woven in displacements. To migrate is also to re-exist: to invent ways of inhabiting from difference, to reclaim dignity in movement, and to overflow the colonial maps that seek to fix bodies in predefined places.
